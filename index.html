AI Assistance Reduces Persistence and Hurts Independent Performance

Grace Liu1, Brian Christian2, Tsvetomira Dumbalska2, Michiel A. Bakker3, Rachit Dubey4
1Carnegie Mellon University   2University of Oxford   3Massachusetts Institute of Technology   4University of California, Los Angeles
arXiv Archive

Abstract

People often optimize for long-term goals in collaboration: A mentor or companion doesn't just answer questions, but also scaffolds learning, tracks progress, and prioritizes the other person's growth over immediate results. In contrast, current AI systems are fundamentally short-sighted collaborators – optimized for providing instant and complete responses, without ever saying no (unless for safety reasons). What are the consequences of this dynamic? Here, through a series of randomized controlled trials on human-AI interactions (N = 1,222), we provide causal evidence for two key consequences of AI assistance: reduced persistence and impairment of unassisted performance. Across a variety of tasks, including mathematical reasoning and reading comprehension, we find that although AI assistance improves performance in the short-term, people perform significantly worse without AI and are more likely to give up. Notably, these effects emerge after only brief interactions with AI (~10 minutes). These findings are particularly concerning because persistence is foundational to skill acquisition and is one of the strongest predictors of long-term learning. We posit that persistence is reduced because AI conditions people to expect immediate answers, thereby denying them the experience of working through challenges on their own. These results suggest the need for AI model development to prioritize scaffolding long-term competence alongside immediate task completion.

Public Significance Statement

The rapid rise of AI chatbots promises immediate and effective help with reasoning-intensive tasks such as studying, writing, coding, and brainstorming. But what happens to users' own abilities when the AI is not available? If people come to rely on AI for everyday problem solving, these tools may quietly erode the very skills they are designed to support. In large-scale experiments involving arithmetic and reading comprehension, we find that AI assistance improves immediate performance but comes at a cognitive cost: after only ~10 minutes of AI-assisted problem-solving, users who lose access to the AI assistant perform worse and give up more frequently than users who never had it. These findings raise urgent questions about the cumulative effects of daily AI use on human persistence and reasoning. Designing AI systems that preserve long-term competency, rather than simply delivering immediate answers, will require fundamentally rethinking how these tools collaborate with people.

Experiment 1: AI Impairs Unassisted Performance and Persistence

To investigate the causal impact of AI assistance on subsequent problem-solving capacity, we conducted a large-scale, randomized controlled experiment (N = 354) on fraction-solving tasks. Participants were randomly assigned to an AI condition or a control condition. In the AI condition, participants solved 12 fraction problems with an AI assistant (GPT-5) available in a sidebar. The AI was then removed without warning, and all participants solved 3 additional test problems independently.

Experiment 2: Replication and Ruling Out Confounds

Experiment 2 replicated our findings (N = 667) with two key methodological improvements: (1) a pretest phase for ability-based exclusions to address potential skill-level confounds from Experiment 1, and (2) a matched sidebar interface for control participants to eliminate interface asymmetry. Despite these controls, we replicated the core effects: AI assistance improved performance during the learning phase but impaired independent performance at test.

Replication of results in Experiment 2 (N = 585 after exclusions). Participants' mean solve rate and skip rate per problem over time with 95% CIs (left), and mean test solve rate and skip rate (right). Participants in the AI condition had a significantly lower solve rate (mean 0.71 vs. 0.77; p = 0.020, Cohen's d = −0.19) than control participants.



Persistence Costs Are Concentrated Among Those Who Obtain Direct Solutions

Analyzing self-reported AI usage patterns, we find that the majority of participants (61%) used AI to get answers directly. These participants showed the largest declines in performance and persistence — not only compared to control participants but also compared to participants who used AI for hints or clarifications. Participants who used AI for hints showed no significant impairments relative to control.

Performance and persistence declines are concentrated among participants who obtained direct solutions from AI. (a) AI usage groups show no significant differences in solve rate or skip rate at pretest, confirming comparable initial skill and motivation levels. (b) Groups differ significantly at test: participants who used AI for direct answers show the lowest solve rate and highest skip rate. (c) Participants who used AI for direct answers show the largest decline in performance and increase in skipping relative to their own pretest, while other groups show similar or improved performance.

Experiment 3: Convergent Evidence from Reading Comprehension

To test whether effects generalize beyond arithmetic, we replicated our design in a reading comprehension task using SAT-style problems (N = 201). Reading comprehension draws on fundamentally different cognitive skills — meaning-making and mental model construction — allowing us to assess the generality of the AI-assistance effect.

Replicating Experiments 1 and 2, AI assistance improved performance during the learning phase, but participants in the AI condition performed significantly worse and were significantly more likely to skip problems once the AI was removed.

Reduced performance and persistence in reading comprehension (N = 168 after exclusions). Participants' mean solve rate and skip rate per problem over time (left) and at test (right). Participants in the AI condition had a significantly lower solve rate (mean 0.76 vs. 0.89; p = 0.007, Cohen's d = −0.42) and higher skip rate (mean 0.08 vs. 0.01; p = 0.008, Cohen's d = 0.42) than control participants.

Key Findings

  • AI assistance reduces persistence: After brief AI-assisted sessions (~10 minutes), participants were significantly more likely to give up on problems once the AI was removed, compared to participants who never had AI assistance.
  • AI assistance impairs independent performance: Across all three experiments, participants who had AI assistance performed significantly worse on unassisted test problems than control participants who never had AI.
  • Effects are driven by direct solution-seeking: Persistence costs were concentrated among participants who prompted AI to solve tasks for them directly. Using AI for hints or clarifications did not produce significant impairments.
  • Effects generalize across domains: The pattern replicated across mathematical reasoning (fraction arithmetic) and reading comprehension, suggesting it is a general consequence of AI-assisted problem solving, not an artifact of any particular task.
  • Brief exposure is sufficient: The effects emerged after only ~10–15 minutes of AI interaction, raising urgent questions about the cumulative effects of daily AI use on long-term human capability.
